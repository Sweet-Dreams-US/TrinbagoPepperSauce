★ Chapter 00 — De Origin ★

From Trinidad
with fire.

A wooden mortar. A scotch bonnet pepper. A grandmother who never measured nothing. That's where this recipe started — in a kitchen in San Fernando, on de southern edge of Trinidad, sometime in de 1970s.

Three generations later, we still make it de same way. We just put it in nicer bottles.

/01

De Recipe.

— San Fernando, ~1972 —

Granny didn't write things down. She'd put de scotch bonnet in de mortar, say "a good handful," then mash it with garlic and shado beni until it smelled right. "Right" was a feeling, not a measurement.

When she'd pour it over fish broth, your eyes would water before de bowl reached de table. When she'd put a drop on a corn dumpling, you'd want a second one before you finished de first. That's de standard.

She'd make it on Sundays. By Wednesday, every cousin in walking distance had a jar. By Friday, somebody was already asking when she'd make more.

That sauce raised three of we.
Now it's raising you.

"We bottled de Caribbean. Carry it home with you."

/02

De Name.

— Trin·ba·go (noun) —

Trinidad. Tobago. Two islands. One country. One sauce.

  • Trin — for de scotch bonnet, de carnival, de calypso.
  • Ba — for de bridge between de two.
  • Go — for de habanero, de beach, de slower pace.
  • Together — Trinbago.

De name picked itself. Because we couldn't pick one island over de other. De recipe came from Trinidad. De peppers came from both. De fire is shared.

/04 — Why It Matter

Real food
come from real people.

Most "Caribbean" hot sauce on shelves never been near de Caribbean. We change that. Every bottle of Trinbago has a recipe with a grandmother's name on it, peppers from a farm we know, and a date stamp from de week it was bottled.

Whether you've never been to Trinidad or you're missing it right now, this is what de island taste like. We didn't invent something new. We just took something old and refused to let it die in a microwave-ready jar.
